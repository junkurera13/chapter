# Chapter experience principles

## Product thesis

Chapter is not a recommendation engine with social features attached.
Experiences are the medium through which Chapter helps people enter life and
connect with other people.

An experience may help someone:

- notice a part of their own world differently;
- deepen a relationship with someone they know;
- meet someone new without browsing a profile;
- become friends through something shared;
- find love as an unpromised outcome of a real encounter.

Chapter does not match profiles. It creates the right experience for two worlds
to meet.

## Design laws

### Design the experience before the place

Start with what happens: an action, rhythm, constraint, shared task, journey, or
ritual. Research may find a place, event, route, class, material, timetable, or
other fact that makes it possible. The researched object is supporting
infrastructure, not automatically the experience.

### Keep one familiar thread and spend one stretch

Choose a grounded thread with emotional or behavioural weight. Preserve enough
of it to create recognition. Stretch exactly one meaningful dimension:

- place;
- activity;
- person;
- time.

Do not make a person absorb an unfamiliar activity, distant place, new person,
and difficult schedule at once.

### Transform rather than repeat

Do not mirror a graph noun directly. Someone who remembers photography does not
need "visit a photography museum" by default. Find the underlying quality:
attention, patience, collecting light, wandering alone, making something
tangible. Build from that mechanism.

### Create story potential

Prefer experiences that may produce a moment with a beginning, a small point of
tension or discovery, and something worth remembering. Avoid manufactured
cinema. A story can be quiet.

### Let evidence carry personalization

Use graph anchors to show why the experience belongs in this world. Do not put
unverifiable claims about the person's past, family, feelings, or preferences
into card copy.

### Make surprise survivable

Surprise should live inside a familiar frame. The person should be able to say
"I would not have found this, but I can see why it is mine."

### Make every card livable

Verify the facts that could prevent action: operating status, hours, booking,
travel, cost, equipment, accessibility, weather dependence, group size, and
the validity window. Provide directions or a concrete way to arrive when the
experience depends on a route.

### Compose the pack, not three isolated ideas

The pack is the product. Its cards must create meaning through contrast:
approachable versus ambitious, solitary versus social, local versus farther,
quiet versus energetic. Three individually good cards may still form a bad
pack.

## Voice

Write like a thoughtful friend making a specific invitation. Be warm, plain,
and unhurried. Avoid marketing language, exclamation marks, forced whimsy,
compatibility claims, autobiographical invention, and explanations of the
machine.
